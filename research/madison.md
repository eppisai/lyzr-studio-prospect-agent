# The seller: why Madison, and who it is for

The brief let me pick any company as the seller. I picked Lyzr, selling [Madison](https://www.madison.lyzr.ai/).

## Why a real Lyzr product

My first build sold a different Lyzr product to small manufacturers. It passed its tests, and I still dropped it: I could not make a believable case that those buyers needed that product. The plumbing worked and the choice of product and buyer did not.

Madison fixed three things at once:

- **Every product claim can be checked.** The agent may only say about Madison what Lyzr's own page says. The analyst has to quote the sentence behind a capability, and the sentence behind its limit.
- **The buyer is narrow and regulated.** Banks and credit unions publish a lot: mergers, earnings, filings, job postings. That gives the researcher real evidence to work with.
- **Qualification matters here.** Madison is new. Not every bank is a good prospect for it, so "is this bank worth an email?" has a real answer.

## What Madison is, from its own page

All of this is from Lyzr's public Madison page, read on 4 and 5 October 2026, put in my own words.

- It is for banks and credit unions.
- It holds a bank's compliance records as one connected model, from the regulation through the obligation, policy, procedure and control to the evidence, and runs agents on that model beside the compliance team.
- Its rule for the agents is short: "Agents propose. Your people decide."
- It never writes back to the bank's core system.
- It does not decide whether the bank is compliant, and it does not certify or guarantee compliance.

In the final runs the analyst quoted the page's own sentence for the capability and for the limit. Those quotes are in the [run records](../tests/transcripts).

## Who it is for right now

Two facts on the page shaped the whole design.

- Madison is taking on a small first group: between three and ten charter institutions, who spend about three months specifying and building it with Lyzr's team and then get the first year free.
- The page speaks directly to institutions approaching $10 billion in assets, where supervision and exam expectations change.

So Madison is looking for a few banks to build with, and it speaks directly to banks near a regulatory size threshold. From that I wrote the campaign's definition of fit, which lives in the [campaign notes](../knowledge-base/MADISON-CAMPAIGN-NOTES.txt):

- **Core fit:** a bank or credit union approaching or recently past a regulatory size threshold, or of similar size, with no dedicated GRC platform team in evidence.
- **Stretch fit:** a much larger bank that already runs a GRC platform with its own engineers. One honest message is worth sending. A full campaign is not.
- **Outside:** not a bank or credit union.

The ideal case is a midsize bank that has just been through a merger or a compliance change. Burke & Herbert is that case in the test set: its parent reported about $11 billion in assets after a merger in May 2026.

These definitions are my campaign choices, drawn from the page. They are not Lyzr's stated sales policy.

## What the agent asks a prospect for

One small thing: to walk one obligation together, from the rule to the evidence behind it. The first message offers no price, pilot, date or integration, and does not mention the charter program.

## Claims the agent does not make

- That Madison certifies or guarantees compliance.
- Any ROI, time saving or customer outcome for Madison.
- Another Lyzr customer's result presented as a Madison result.
- Regulatory or legal advice.
- That any named bank uses Madison. Lyzr's site mentions BMO in another context, and the agent treats that as a reason to check with the account owner, nothing more.
